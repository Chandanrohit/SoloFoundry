# Pricing and unit economics

**Maturity:** reviewed method guide; no price or willingness-to-pay estimate has been validated for a real product.

Pricing is a product and business decision: it affects who can adopt, what users expect, support workload, and whether the product can sustain delivery. A spreadsheet cannot reveal willingness to pay by itself. Combine cost knowledge with buyer research, alternatives, observed behavior, and ethical pricing tests.

## Separate four questions

1. **Value:** what outcome does the customer receive, and how important is it?
2. **Packaging:** which capabilities/limits belong in each plan for a coherent use case?
3. **Price metric:** what unit scales with value and is easy for a buyer to predict (seat, project, usage, transaction, or flat fee)?
4. **Price point:** what amount can the target buyer accept while supporting a sustainable business?

Do not choose a usage metric simply because it is easy to meter. Surprise bills, unpredictable quotas, and opaque limits erode trust. Explain overages, cancellation, refunds, taxes, and what happens to data when a plan ends.

## Learn before setting a public price

Ask about the last time the problem occurred, current spending/workarounds, who approves a purchase, and what a failed solution costs. Ask what budget or procurement process actually applies. Hypothetical “would you pay?” answers are weak evidence; use pilots, paid trials, letters of intent, or purchases where appropriate and clearly disclose what is being tested.

Map alternatives and price anchors with dated sources. Do not copy a competitor's price without understanding audience, service level, costs, and positioning. Test a small number of transparent packages; avoid deceptive “was/now” pricing or fake scarcity. The [SBA planning guide](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/plan-your-business/) links to startup-cost and break-even resources; these are estimates, not a substitute for bookkeeping or actual results.

## Estimate contribution per customer

For a simple subscription estimate:

`monthly contribution per account = collected revenue − payment/refund costs − variable infrastructure/API costs − variable support/service costs`

`break-even accounts = monthly fixed costs ÷ monthly contribution per account`

Example (hypothetical): fixed costs of $600/month and contribution of $20/account imply 30 accounts to cover those fixed costs, before owner compensation, tax, debt, and irregular expenses. State currency, assumptions, and period. If contribution is zero/negative, scaling usage increases the loss.

Include free-to-paid conversion, failed payments, refunds, discounts, annual prepayment obligations, churn, and usage spikes. Keep a low/base/high scenario and test sensitivity to the two assumptions that matter most. Do not treat gross revenue, ARR, or lifetime value estimates as cash in hand.

## Unit economics without false precision

Useful ratios include gross margin by plan, acquisition cost by channel, activation-to-paid conversion, revenue retention, and payback period. Define numerators, time windows, cohorts, and attribution rules. At low volume, report actual counts next to rates (e.g., 2 of 12), and avoid extrapolating a few early customers into stable lifetime value.
