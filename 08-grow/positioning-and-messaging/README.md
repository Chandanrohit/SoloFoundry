# Positioning and messaging

**Maturity:** reviewed method guide; no positioning or message has been tested with a real market.

Positioning helps a specific buyer understand whether a product is for them, what outcome it offers, and why it is a credible choice among alternatives. It is a set of testable hypotheses, not a clever tagline exercise. Begin with the [customer research](../../01-discover/customer-research/README.md), [segment analysis](../../01-discover/market-intelligence/README.md), and [product brief](../../templates/PRODUCT_BRIEF.md).

## Write a positioning hypothesis

Fill in this sentence with concrete language:

> For **[specific people in a situation]** who need **[job/outcome]**, **[product]** helps them **[measurable or observable result]**. Unlike **[current alternative]**, it **[credible difference]** because **[evidence/capability]**.

If the audience is “everyone,” the outcome is “save time,” or the difference is a feature list, return to user interviews and observed workflows. A positioning hypothesis should name a reachable group and a painful moment, not a demographic stereotype.

## Map the alternatives

Users compare your product to more than direct competitors: spreadsheets, a manual process, a colleague, another budget line, or doing nothing. For each alternative, capture:

| Alternative | When users choose it | What they value | Cost/risk they accept | Evidence source |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Use observed behavior and actual language, not only competitor websites. Mark estimates and unknowns. Revisit the map when the buyer, workflow, or purchasing context changes.

## Turn the hypothesis into a message

Build a simple page or short pitch in this order:

1. **Audience + problem:** show that you recognize the exact situation.
2. **Outcome:** state what changes for the user, without unsupported claims.
3. **How it works:** show the workflow or proof, not a pile of adjectives.
4. **Evidence:** product demo, transparent method, customer quote with permission, or measured result with context.
5. **Trade-offs:** state constraints such as supported integrations, data handling, or who should not use it.
6. **Next step:** one low-friction action suited to the user's readiness.

Keep a claim ledger: exact claim, evidence, scope, date, owner, and where it appears. Marketing claims should be truthful and substantiated; the [FTC advertising guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing) is a U.S. source, and other jurisdictions have their own requirements. Do not turn an individual testimonial into a typical-results claim without support.

## Test messages without confusing interest with demand

Test comprehension first: ask a target user what the product does, for whom, and what they would do next after reading the page. Then test behavior through a small channel experiment. Record the audience, source, exposure, action, and follow-up. A click or compliment is weak evidence of willingness to adopt or pay; an observed workflow change, pilot commitment, or purchase is stronger but still context-specific.

Avoid changing audience, offer, and channel at the same time. Use the [growth experiment template](../../templates/GROWTH_EXPERIMENT.md) and the discovery [experiment log](../../templates/EXPERIMENT_LOG.md).
