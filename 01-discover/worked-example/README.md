# Worked example: missed handoffs in a small repair shop

> **Status:** illustrative teaching case, not empirical research. The people, observations, numbers, and outcomes below are hypothetical and must not be cited as market evidence.

## Starting signal

Imagine a builder hears from one independent bicycle repair shop that staff sometimes lose track of repair updates between intake and pickup. This is a lead only. It says nothing about how often it happens, whether it is costly, whether other shops share the problem, or whether software is the right answer.

## Discovery plan

The builder writes a problem brief and lists risky assumptions:

1. Handoffs regularly cause avoidable customer calls or delayed repairs.
2. The shop owner or service manager considers the impact worth addressing.
3. Staff can use a change without adding more work at intake.
4. A small shop can adopt a solution without a costly point-of-sale integration.
5. A reachable group of shops has similar workflows and a buyer who can decide.

The first test is to interview several people from different roles at independent shops, recruited outside the builder's immediate network. The purpose is to map recent repair workflows and seek counterexamples, not to pitch an app. The number is a convenience for this example, not a statistically meaningful sample-size recommendation.

## Hypothetical observations

Suppose two staff describe a recent delayed status update, while another says their existing paper tags and daily review work reliably. The first two cite customer callbacks but cannot estimate their frequency. Nobody has yet paid to solve the issue. These observations support more inquiry into workflow variation; they do not establish prevalence or willingness to pay.

## Next tests

Observe a normal intake-to-pickup workflow with consent, avoiding customer-identifying data. Compare current workarounds and count handoffs or callbacks over a defined period with a clear denominator. If the friction is visible, test a paper or no-code status board with staff before building software. Then ask the owner to commit time or payment to a bounded pilot; record setup and support effort as well as use.

Set a decision rule before the pilot. For example, proceed to a narrow product test only if the target shops complete the workflow repeatedly, staff can maintain the status data, and at least one buyer agrees to a paid or otherwise consequential pilot. The actual thresholds must be chosen with participating shops and reflect the cost and risk of the product. A weak result should lead to a workflow change, another segment, or a stop decision—not a more flattering metric.

## What this example teaches

- A specific complaint creates a research question, not a product specification.
- Different roles and contexts can have different workflows.
- A prototype tests task usability; it does not prove a market.
- Adoption, willingness to pay, operational cost, and repeatability are separate assumptions.
- The case has no external data. All example observations and proposed thresholds are hypothetical.
