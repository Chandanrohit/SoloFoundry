# Jobs and workflows

> **Status:** reviewed synthesis. Use it to structure inquiry, not to claim a universal theory of customer behavior.

## Describe the job before proposing a feature

A job is progress someone is trying to make in a particular situation. Describe the actor, trigger, desired progress, constraints, and current way of coping. Keep the observation separate from the proposed product: “operations staff reconcile mismatched orders before the daily cutoff” is more useful than “they need an AI dashboard.”

## Map the current workflow

For one recent instance, capture:

- Trigger and desired outcome.
- Steps, tools, handoffs, decision makers, and waiting time.
- Inputs and outputs, including data quality and access constraints.
- Errors, rework, exceptions, and consequences.
- Current workaround, including manual labor, spreadsheets, agencies, existing software, and doing nothing.
- Who experiences the friction, who pays, who chooses, and who could block adoption.

Use a simple sequence diagram or table. Do not infer frequency from one walkthrough. Compare several cases and note where the workflow differs by role, organization, or context.

## Separate functional, emotional, and social context

People may want a task completed, reduce uncertainty or risk, or meet expectations of colleagues and customers. Treat these as questions to explore, not categories to force into every interview. Ask what success and failure mean in the participant's own terms.

## Output

Write a concise job statement and workflow map, then list the observed friction, evidence source, open questions, and segments where the pattern may differ. Link each claim to notes or an evidence record that can be safely shared. Avoid storing identifiable interview data in the public repository.
