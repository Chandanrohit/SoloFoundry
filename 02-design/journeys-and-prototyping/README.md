# Journeys and prototypes

> **Status:** reviewed decision guide. A map is a hypothesis about a journey until checked with users and operational evidence.

## Map the whole task

Start before and continue after the screen or feature you plan to build. Map the user's trigger, goal, steps, channels, people, information needs, handoffs, decisions, waiting, exceptions, and recovery. Include support and operational work behind the interface. Identify where a user leaves one system or needs help from another person.

Use a simple table for a narrow task and a journey map when multiple steps, channels, roles, or organizations matter. Annotate each step with its evidence source and confidence. Include different paths for people with different needs rather than forcing every person into one “typical” route.

## Decide where a product change belongs

The journey may reveal that the best improvement is clearer information, a policy or process change, a better handoff, an integration, assisted support, or no new software. Check who owns each step and whether a proposed change moves burden or risk to another user or team.

## Prototype the uncertainty

Choose the lowest-fidelity artifact that can answer the current question: a paper sketch for task order, a clickable wireframe for navigation, a scripted service walkthrough for handoffs, or a working slice when technical behavior itself is uncertain. State the hypothesis, target participants, task, success signals, and what the prototype cannot test.

Make it clear to participants when an artifact is simulated. Do not collect real credentials, sensitive data, or production information in a prototype. Label fake or sample data and prevent a test interface from sending real messages, charging money, or taking consequential actions.

## Check the map and prototype together

Give participants a believable goal without telling them which control to choose. Observe where they pause, what they expect, what they misunderstand, and whether they recover. Ask follow-up questions after the task; do not treat “I like it” as evidence of successful use. Compare observations with the journey and update both when new context appears.
