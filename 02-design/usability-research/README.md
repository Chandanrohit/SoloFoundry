# Usability research

> **Status:** reviewed practical guide. Small qualitative tests find issues and explain behavior; they do not estimate population-wide success rates.

## Choose the question and participants

Decide whether you need to learn if users can find, understand, complete, or recover from a task. Recruit people who represent the relevant use context and include participants likely to encounter access barriers. Existing customers, colleagues, and friends can be useful for early feedback but may not reflect the intended audience. Record recruitment source, context, accessibility setup, and incentives.

## Prepare a low-bias session

Write a short, neutral introduction, consent and recording plan, tasks, follow-up prompts, and a note-taking format. A task should describe a goal rather than reveal the interface path. Avoid teaching or rescuing participants unless the protocol calls for it; note when help is needed. Make participation voluntary, protect recordings and notes, and explain who will access them and when they will be deleted.

Use the [usability test plan](../../templates/USABILITY_TEST_PLAN.md). If a product affects health, finances, legal rights, employment, children, or other high-impact areas, assess applicable research, privacy, safety, and oversight requirements before recruiting or testing.

## Facilitate and observe

Ask the participant to work through realistic tasks and say what they expect where appropriate. Watch for completion, wrong turns, hesitation, abandonment, errors, workarounds, and recovery. Do not infer a cause from one behavior; ask an open follow-up after the task. If the session is remote, check device, connection, assistive technology, and privacy constraints in advance.

Do not interpret a small number of sessions as a statistical measure. The number needed depends on the research question, the diversity of users and workflows, and the consequences of missing an issue. Repeat or broaden the study when important groups or failure paths remain unseen.

## Turn findings into changes

Summarize the observed behavior, task and context, impact, evidence, uncertainty, and suggested next step. Separate participant words from researcher interpretation. Prioritize issues by how they block user goals, risk harm or exclusion, and whether they recur across contexts. Preserve disagreements and verify proposed fixes with another test or accessibility review.
